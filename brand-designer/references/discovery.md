# Discovery and strategy

Use supplied answers and existing materials before asking questions. A short, focused intake is usually enough to draft a brief; collect deeper detail only when it changes the design.

## Inputs that change the design

- Business/product name, offer, stage, geography, languages, and relationship to parent or sister brands.
- Primary audience, problem addressed, buying context, value proposition, and credible differentiators.
- Desired perception and personality; examples of what the brand should and should not feel like.
- Competitors and references: record which qualities to learn from and which to avoid copying.
- Existing name, logo, palette, fonts, voice, guidelines, ownership, and which decisions are approved or open.
- Priority channels and applications, practical deadlines, accessibility needs, print requirements, and asset formats.
- Who approves decisions and whether any stages have already been approved or explicitly delegated.

When important inputs are unknown, propose labeled assumptions for approval. Do not invent company history, customer research, certifications, or product claims. Naming is optional when a name is established; name suggestions and domain observations do not constitute trademark clearance.

## Strategy brief for the first gate

Write a concise business summary, audience and need, competitive context, positioning statement, brand promise and evidence, personality attributes with behavioral implications, messaging priorities, design constraints, and success criteria. List open questions and the proposed deliverables. Include source links for research and identify user-provided statements separately.

For positioning, a useful structure is: For [audience] who need [job], [brand] offers [category/benefit], supported by [credible reason]. Use it as a thinking aid, not mandatory final copy.

Ask approval of the concrete brief and proposed assumptions. Record what is approved and leave unresolved items open. If the user supplied an already approved strategy, confirm its scope from the evidence and proceed without restarting discovery.

## Evidence and acceptance checklist

- Record what is user-provided, externally researched, inferred or proposed; cite sources and retrieval dates for material market and competitor claims.
- Define the specific buyer problem, purchasing context, credible differentiators, brand promise and operational evidence required to support it. Do not present untested audiences or price points as validated.
- Document geography, language and cultural considerations, accessibility, priority physical/digital touchpoints, practical budgets, production constraints, deadlines and approver.
- Establish measurable success tests appropriate to the project (for example recognition, menu comprehension, readability, consistency, or customer testing); distinguish intended targets from achieved outcomes.
- End the strategy brief with explicit assumptions, unresolved decisions, recommended scope and approval question. Record approval scope before dependent naming/visual decisions.
